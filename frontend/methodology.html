Methodology & Postmortem

How the numbers on the dashboard are made, and which earlier claims did not survive measurement

Every number the dashboard shows is computed live by the models described here. This page is the long-form companion to the project README: the methods, the measured results, and the record of what was wrong before it was fixed. Corrections are kept in place rather than edited out.

Figures are those recorded in the repository README at the time of writing. Where this page and the README disagree, the README is the source of truth.

1. What you are looking at 2. How it works 3. Results 4. What I got wrong 5. Limitations 6. Links

1. What you are looking at

An Asian option is a contract whose payout depends on the average price of a stock over the contract's life rather than only its final price, and there is no formula for its fair value. The standard way to price one is to simulate a large number of possible futures and average the payout, which is accurate but slow: at a 100,000-path budget a single price takes roughly 100 milliseconds, and a trading desk needs thousands of prices and their risk sensitivities refreshed continuously. This project trains a neural network on those simulated prices so that it learns the pricing function itself, and it then answers in about 714 microseconds, roughly 500 times faster, with a measured error of 1.4 basis points of strike. Because the network is a smooth function, its risk sensitivities (the Greeks) come out as exact derivatives of the network instead of by nudging each input and re-pricing, which is what makes the result usable for hedging. The dashboard shows the network's answer next to a fresh simulation with its confidence interval so you can check it yourself, and the postmortem below lists the places where earlier claims about this project were wrong and how each error was found.

2. How it works

One model for every strike

An Asian call's price scales linearly in spot and strike: multiply both by the same factor and the price scales with them. So the network only learns the unit-strike price as a function of moneyness, maturity, volatility and rate, f(S/K, T, σ, r), and any contract is priced as K · f(S/K, T, σ, r). One model covers every strike exactly, and puts come from Asian put-call parity, which is also exact. The test suite checks parity for both the Monte Carlo engine and the surrogate.

The network, the training signal, and the ensemble

The pricer is a residual multilayer perceptron with SiLU activations and LayerNorm, about 134k parameters. Smooth activations matter because the Greeks are derivatives of the network; a ReLU network would have zero gamma almost everywhere. Five independently initialized networks are averaged as a deep ensemble, which lowers error, and the Greeks average cleanly through the mean.

Training follows differential machine learning (Huge and Savine, 2020): the pathwise delta and vega are computed on the same Monte Carlo paths that produce each price, for almost no extra cost, and the network is fit to the prices and their derivatives together in a variance-normalized loss. A controlled ablation at the same sample budget, prices only versus the differential loss, cut delta error about 3x and vega error about 4x. The served model was trained on 500,000 labels of 5,000 paths each for 400 epochs. Label generation runs on the GPU in float64 at 0.85 G path-steps/s on an RTX 5080, so the full set takes 148 s instead of roughly 80 minutes on CPU; float64 is not optional, because the same kernel at float32 with identical seeds injects 10.03 bps of price RMSE at 5,000 paths.

Greeks by automatic differentiation

All five Greeks are exact derivatives of the network obtained by automatic differentiation, not finite differences, and the tests check them against finite-difference perturbations. A price alone costs about 714 µs at p50. Price plus all five Greeks costs 8.4 ms at p50 (9.3 ms p95, 9.8 ms p99), because gamma needs a second backward pass through each of the five ensemble members. Batch throughput is 487,843 prices/s at a batch of 10,000. Serving stays on the CPU: at a batch of one the GPU is slower (1,813 µs versus 1,336 µs), being kernel-launch bound.

Monte Carlo: antithetic sampling and a geometric control variate

The reference engine, which generates the training labels and the live comparison price on the dashboard, uses antithetic sampling with a geometric-Asian control variate (Kemna and Vorst, 1990). The geometric-average option has a closed-form price, so the simulation error on it can be measured and subtracted from the arithmetic estimate. Measured as the ratio of empirical standard deviations across 300 seeded replications, the control variate cuts the standard error by about 24x (24.0x at 5,000 paths, 24.5x at 20,000). On the dashboard the benchmark switches regime automatically: Asian under geometric Brownian motion above 12 trading days to expiry, rough Bergomi at or below.

Same-day expiry: a rough Bergomi surrogate with exact Volterra simulation

Very short-dated option smiles show a power-law skew that classical models cannot reproduce, so a separate five-member ensemble serves maturities of 12 trading days or less, trained on a rough Bergomi Monte Carlo engine. The driver is the Riemann-Liouville Volterra process of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral (2016), W̃_t = √(2H) ∫₀ᵗ (t−s)^(H−½) dW_s, with Hurst index near 0.1, simulated exactly from the joint Gaussian law of the driving Brownian motion and the Volterra integral; the scheme is verified against quadrature to 5.4e-08 and against 400,000 draws. On 400 held-out points against 500,000-path references the 0DTE ensemble measures 1.48 bps RMSE with +0.13 bps bias, below the 2.35 bps noise floor of its own 20,000-path training labels. The served checkpoint is currently uncalibrated, for the reason given in the postmortem.

Deep hedging and the CVaR objective

Following Buehler and coauthors (2019), a policy network maps the hedging state to a position and is trained to minimize the 95% conditional value at risk of the terminal loss, with transaction costs inside the objective. The dashboard simulates 3,000 paths of hedging a short option to expiry and shows the learned policy's profit-and-loss distribution next to a Black-Scholes delta hedge on the same paths. Out of sample, the learned policy loses to a vol-matched delta hedge and to a cost-aware Whalley-Wilmott no-trade band; that is a negative result and it is reported as one, in the results and in the postmortem.

Explainability by integrated gradients

The feature-attribution panel runs Integrated Gradients through the ensemble against an at-the-money baseline, so each input is credited with its share of the price difference from that baseline. The completeness check, that the attributions sum to the price difference, is reported alongside, so a bad attribution shows up as a visible residual rather than disappearing into the chart.

Real market data

Everything above runs on simulated data. The exception is a live Deribit option chain: 836 BTC instruments across 12 expiries, fetched from public endpoints with no API key and committed as a snapshot so the diagnostics run offline. Reading the coin-denominated premium on the per-expiry forward and inverting Black-76 reproduces Deribit's own published mark IV to a median of 0.0028 vol points; reading it as a dollar price instead gives 4.62 vol points where the truth is 32.88, wrong by 7.1x and still a smooth, plausible surface. Of 51 static-arbitrage violations that appear on mid prices, zero are executable once the actual bid-ask has to be crossed.

4. What I got wrong and how I found out

Each entry records the claim as it was made, how the error surfaced, and what changed. Most were found by audit rather than by a failing test, which is itself a finding: the tests checked the things that were easy to state, and the errors lived in the things that were easy to assume.

The deep hedger did not beat delta hedging

The claim

That the learned hedger "reduced 95% tail loss by roughly 30% versus delta hedging." Retracted.

How it surfaced

The comparison was measured in-sample, against a handicapped baseline, on a measure that was not a valid pricing measure. Three separate problems, all measured:

  1. The simulated measure was not risk-neutral. risk_neutralize matched per-step marginals but left cross-step covariance free, so paths realized 1.28x the requested volatility and discounted spot was not a martingale: E[S_T] exceeded e^{rT} by up to 147 bps. The option was booked at Black-Scholes while being worth about 30% more under the measure actually being simulated.
  2. The baseline was handicapped, hedging at the requested σ while the paths realized 1.28σ. On the old measure, vol-matching the baseline alone closed about 83% of the claimed gap.
  3. The comparison was in-sample on a degenerate generator. The WGAN market simulator is mode-collapsed (participation ratio 4.66 of 30 factors), which makes its paths forecastable: R² = 0.8755 regressing future returns on realized ones, versus 0.0006 for GBM. Minimizing CVaR on such paths rewards market timing, not hedging.
What changed

Terminal variance and the martingale condition are now enforced exactly and the premium is priced under the simulated measure. A cost-aware Whalley-Wilmott baseline was added, and the evaluation moved out of sample onto risk-neutral GBM over a 12-cell grid. compare() reports both measures with bootstrap standard errors and defaults its headline to the out-of-sample one. The headline is now the negative result in the table above, and the dashboard's hedging panel and risk report follow the measured direction rather than the old claim.

The rough volatility driver was the wrong process

The claim

That the 0DTE surrogate agreed with its rough Bergomi teacher to about 2 basis points. The agreement was real; the teacher was wrong.

How it surfaced

rough_vol.py built the Type-I (Mandelbrot-Van Ness) fractional Brownian covariance 0.5(t_i^2H + t_j^2H − |t_i−t_j|^2H). Rough Bergomi is driven by the Riemann-Liouville Volterra process W̃_t = √(2H) ∫₀ᵗ (t−s)^(H−½) dW_s. The two agree on the diagonal, both giving Var[W̃_t] = t^2H, which is why the martingale property held and nothing looked wrong, and they agree nowhere else: at H = 0.1172 the maximum off-diagonal relative difference is 4.93, and corr(W̃_t1, W̃_t50) was +0.320 against a true +0.054.

There was a second half. A Cholesky factor of the covariance is not the Volterra kernel, because W̃ is a continuous stochastic integral rather than a linear function of n coarse increments. Factorising the covariance alone forces corr(Z_1, W̃_t1) = 1 by construction when the truth is √(2H)/(H+½) = 0.7844, so the leverage correlation ρ was being applied to the wrong object, over-correlating spot and volatility precisely at the short end where a 0DTE skew fit is identified.

What changed

Both defects are fixed with the exact joint-Gaussian scheme, verified against quadrature to 5.4e-08 and against 400,000 draws. The 0DTE checkpoint was regenerated against the corrected driver, the old one is kept as model_0dte_legacy_wrong_kernel.pt for comparison, and the old figure was replaced by a measurement against the right teacher: 1.48 bps RMSE on 400 held-out points against 500,000-path references.

The calibration was not live

The claim

That the 0DTE model was "calibrated to the live SPY smile ... about 2 volatility points across 72 quotes and two expiries."

How it surfaced

The committed artifacts/rough_calibration.json reads as_of 2026-07-09T03:43:16-04:00, which is 03:43 in New York with the market closed, with quote_source: "last_trade_market_closed" and accepted: true. Those exact parameters (H 0.1172, η 2.1384, ρ −0.7387) are what the served model_0dte.pt carried. A market-closed fit passed the quality gate and was trained into the served model because the gate tested only RMSE and bound-pinning and never looked at staleness. Related: in the market-closed branch, time-to-expiry was stamped from now while the prices were the previous session's last trades, which on synthetic quotes with known truth inflated √ξ by 21% and moved H by 0.021.

What changed

The 0DTE checkpoint has been regenerated on the corrected driver and is labelled as it is: uncalibrated, because the prior calibration was fitted under the old kernel from market-closed quotes. A live calibration under the corrected kernel is the outstanding step, not a claimed one.

The 1.4 bp error was not a noise floor; it was a Softplus bias

The claim

That the 1.4 bp price RMSE was the label-noise floor and therefore irreducible.

How it surfaced

On 600 held-out points, 89.3% of price errors were positive and the mean accounted for 47.6% of total MSE. Bucketing by true price magnitude showed a flat additive offset (+1.05, +1.03, +1.06, +1.20, +0.89 bps across five decades of price), and where the true price is below 0.01 bps the network still predicted about 1.08 bps and never went below 0.898. The cause is the output layer: nn.Softplus() cannot emit zero, so it floors at about 1 bp and lifts the whole surface. The initialization made it worse, starting every run at softplus(0) = 0.693, which is 6,930 bps against a mean price of 3,664 bps.

What changed

Two fixes were tested at full scale, 500,000 labels of 5,000 paths, 400 epochs, five-member ensembles, both arms identical except for the one change under test:

Head conditioning, which carries the output magnitude in a fixed scale with the Softplus head initialized near unity, halved the systematic bias and is the served checkpoint. The residual-over-geometric idea did not work, and that is a real result: it made things 37% worse, because the residual has a 3.8x wider relative dynamic range (p99/p50 of 12.46 versus 3.27) and that outweighs its 21x smaller output scale.

What the promotion cost is stated rather than buried. In a paired comparison on the same 1,500 points against the same references, price RMSE improved 10.8% (1.531 to 1.366 bps) and the bias halved (+1.032 to +0.468), but delta got 5.9% worse (7.338e-4 to 7.768e-4) and vega 1.2% worse (17.757e-4 to 17.978e-4). Promotion is now gated by scripts/promote_model.py, which re-prices a fresh 1,500-point test set against 200,000-path references on a seed used by neither training nor the ablation and writes model.pt only if the candidate beats the incumbent on both RMSE and |bias|; the first version of the gate tested price only, so it could not have seen the Greeks regression. The Levy comparison corroborates the diagnosis from an independent direction: the near-zero-price floor shrank from 1.050 to 0.310 bps when the head was conditioned.

One more caveat: the absolute RMSE is heavy-tailed enough to move with the test draw. The same promoted checkpoint measures 1.301, 1.329, 1.366 and 1.488 bps on four independent Latin-hypercube draws, which is why the paired comparison is the meaningful one.

The standard error was overstated by about 45%

The claim

That the control variate cut the Monte Carlo standard error by "about 30x."

How it surfaced

The 30x came from the estimator's own reported standard error, which was computed as if the antithetic pairs were independent draws. They are not, and treating them that way overstated the true error by about 45%.

What changed

Both the estimator and the claim were fixed. Measured properly, as the ratio of empirical standard deviations across 300 seeded replications, the reduction is 24.0x at 5,000 paths and 24.5x at 20,000. The confidence interval on the dashboard is the corrected one.

The latency claim was off by 8x

The claim

"Roughly a millisecond for a single price plus all Greeks."

How it surfaced

Measuring it. About 714 µs is the price-only figure. Price plus all Greeks is 8.4 ms at p50, because gamma needs a double backward pass across five ensemble members.

What changed

The two figures are reported separately, with percentiles, and the batch-throughput number (487,843 prices/s at a batch of 10,000) is stated as the thing that does hold up.

The live demo was killed by its own Monte Carlo

August 2026

What happened

At the time, the public deployment ran on Render's free tier: a 512 MB container with 0.1 CPU. After a routine deploy the service crash-looped with exit code 137, which is the kernel's out-of-memory killer. The container booted, because PyTorch's import is a fixed floor of about 300 MB and that fits. The first page view did not survive.

How it surfaced

The dashboard's page load fired several Monte Carlo endpoints at once, and a single request's float64 path matrices peaked near 480 MB: the one-shot engine held three full (paths × steps) matrices alive at the same time, measured at 483 MB on the convergence endpoint's unconditional 400,000-path reference run. On a developer machine with tens of gigabytes that never registered as a cost. On a fixed 512 MB box, any allocation that scales with a request parameter is a crash waiting for the right input.

What changed

Both Monte Carlo engines now simulate in fixed-size blocks of paths, and only the one-dimensional per-path payoffs outlive a block, so peak memory is a constant that no longer depends on the path count. Measured at 400,000 paths: 483 MB before, 46 MB after. The seeded results are bit-identical to the one-shot version, because the blocked draws continue the same generator stream and the payoffs are reassembled in the same order, so no number on the dashboard moved. Alongside it, the API now admits one simulation job at a time and queues the rest, the convergence reference dropped from 400,000 to 100,000 paths, and the dashboard loads its heavy panels sequentially instead of concurrently. The lesson is unglamorous: on a small box, bound every allocation by a constant and measure the peak, not the average.

5. Honest limitations

  • This is a research and portfolio project, not production trading infrastructure. Nothing here is investment advice.
  • The pricer takes a single flat volatility rather than a full surface, and there is no arbitrage-free constraint built into the learned surface.
  • The rough volatility model uses a fixed Hurst index rather than calibrating it jointly with the smile, and the served 0DTE checkpoint is currently uncalibrated: its last calibration was fitted under the old kernel from market-closed quotes.
  • The deep hedger does not beat a properly specified baseline out of sample, and the WGAN market simulator it was originally trained on is mode-collapsed, so it is not a sound measure for evaluating a hedging policy.
  • Promoting the conditioned-head pricer improved price accuracy but made the served delta 5.9% and vega 1.2% worse; a service hedging off these Greeks should weigh that differently than a service pricing with them.
  • The absolute price RMSE moves with the test draw (1.301 to 1.488 bps across four independent draws); only paired comparisons on identical points and references are treated as meaningful.
  • Market data for the ticker feature comes from yfinance, which is retail-grade and has stale or missing quotes. The Deribit chain is a committed snapshot, not a live feed.
  • The live demo now runs on a small always-on instance, so there is no wake-up delay. A free-tier deploy of the same image fits in 512 MB only because of the blocked Monte Carlo described in the postmortem, and at 0.1 CPU the convergence chart takes several seconds to fill.
