Methodology

How the numbers on the dashboard are made

Every number the dashboard shows is computed live by the models described here: the methods, how each figure was measured, and the limits of what the models cover.

Figures are produced by the evaluation scripts in the repository, linked at the end.

1. What you are looking at 2. How it works 3. Results 4. Scope 5. Links

1. What you are looking at

An Asian option is a contract whose payout depends on the average price of a stock over the contract's life rather than only its final price, and there is no exact formula for its fair value. The standard way to price one is to simulate a large number of possible futures and average the payout, which is accurate but slow: the 400,000-path reference behind the benchmark table below takes 480 milliseconds for a single price, and a trading desk needs thousands of prices and their risk sensitivities refreshed continuously. This project trains a neural network on those simulated prices so that it learns the pricing function itself, and it then answers in about 633 microseconds, roughly 750 times faster, with a measured error of 1.3 basis points of strike against 200,000-path references. Because the network is a smooth function, its risk sensitivities (the Greeks) come out as exact derivatives of the network in the same call, instead of by nudging each input and re-pricing; delta and vega are fitted to, and measured against, pathwise Monte Carlo derivatives, and section 3 gives their error. The dashboard shows the network's answer next to a fresh simulation with its confidence interval so you can check it yourself.

2. How it works

One model for every strike

An Asian call's price scales linearly in spot and strike: multiply both by the same factor and the price scales with them. So the network only learns the unit-strike price as a function of moneyness, maturity, volatility and rate, f(S/K, T, σ, r), and any contract is priced as K · f(S/K, T, σ, r). One model covers every strike exactly, and puts come from Asian put-call parity, which is also exact. The test suite checks parity for both the Monte Carlo engine and the surrogate.

The network, the training signal, and the ensemble

The pricer is a residual multilayer perceptron with SiLU activations and LayerNorm, about 134k parameters. Smooth activations matter because the Greeks are derivatives of the network; a ReLU network would have zero gamma almost everywhere. Five independently initialized networks are averaged as a deep ensemble, which lowers error, and the Greeks average cleanly through the mean.

Training follows differential machine learning (Huge and Savine, 2020): the pathwise delta and vega are computed on the same Monte Carlo paths that produce each price, for almost no extra cost, and the network is fit to the prices and their derivatives together in a variance-normalized loss. A controlled ablation at the same sample budget, prices only versus the differential loss, cut delta error about 3x and vega error about 4x. The served model was trained on 500,000 labels of 5,000 paths each for 400 epochs. Label generation runs on the GPU in float64 at 0.85 G path-steps/s on an RTX 5080, so the full set takes 148 s instead of roughly 80 minutes on CPU; float64 is not optional, because the same kernel at float32 with identical seeds injects 10.03 bps of price RMSE at 5,000 paths.

Greeks by automatic differentiation

All five Greeks are exact derivatives of the network obtained by automatic differentiation, not finite differences. The test suite checks four of them: delta, vega and theta against finite-difference perturbations of the network, and rho against the closed-form parity derivative at three contracts, to 1e-6. Gamma is measured on the same 600 held-out contracts against a conditional-Monte-Carlo reference: the arithmetic average is linear in the spot, so gamma is the discounted density of the scaled average at the strike, and conditioning on the first increment gives that density in closed form with no kernel and no bandwidth (it reproduces Black-Scholes gamma to machine precision when the contract has one fixing). The ensemble's gamma error is 140 × 10−4 RMSE per unit of (S/K)2, of which the reference's own Monte Carlo noise accounts for 51 × 10−4 RMS; where gamma is large (Black-Scholes-scale gamma above 0.5, 228 contracts) the median relative error is 0.6 %. The error concentrates at small σ√T, the same hinge region the arbitrage audit identifies: 340-420 × 10−4 RMSE below σ√T = 0.1 against 75 above 0.2. They are quoted per share of the underlying: delta per $1 of spot, gamma per $1 squared, vega and rho per point of volatility and of rate (1 % = 1 point), and theta per trading day, 252 to a year. Every maturity on the site is in the same trading days, so decay per day times days to expiry is the premium of the contract as quoted. Theta is the derivative with respect to maturity, and an Asian's 50 fixing dates are spread over its life, so shortening the maturity moves the averaging window as well as the time left: it is a tenor sensitivity, not calendar decay on a fixed schedule. A price alone costs about 633 µs in the benchmark below. Price plus all five Greeks costs roughly twelve times that, because gamma needs a second backward pass through each of the five ensemble members. Serving is CPU-only in every code path here, and the dashboard reports the latency of each request it actually served.

Monte Carlo: antithetic sampling and a geometric control variate

The reference engine, which generates the training labels and the live comparison price on the dashboard, uses antithetic sampling with a geometric-Asian control variate (Kemna and Vorst, 1990). The geometric-average option has a closed-form price, so the simulation error on it can be measured and subtracted from the arithmetic estimate. Measured as the ratio of empirical standard deviations across 300 seeded replications, the control variate cuts the standard error by about 24x (24.0x at 5,000 paths, 24.5x at 20,000). On the dashboard the benchmark switches regime automatically: Asian under geometric Brownian motion above 12 trading days to expiry, rough Bergomi at or below.

Same-day expiry: a rough Bergomi surrogate with exact Volterra simulation

Very short-dated option smiles show a power-law skew that classical models cannot reproduce, so a separate five-member ensemble serves maturities of 12 trading days or less, trained on a rough Bergomi Monte Carlo engine. The driver is the Riemann-Liouville Volterra process of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral (2016), W̃_t = √(2H) ∫₀ᵗ (t−s)^(H−½) dW_s, simulated exactly from the joint Gaussian law of the driving Brownian motion and the Volterra integral; the scheme is verified against quadrature to 5.4e-08 and against 400,000 draws. The served ensemble is trained on the dynamics of a live SPY calibration: on 2026-08-20 the calibrator fitted η = 3.66, ρ = −0.63 and H = 0.26 jointly with the forward variance to 618 live mid quotes across seven expiries at 1.57 vol points of implied-vol RMSE, the fit passed the quality gate, and the checkpoint records that provenance in its own metadata. Its ensemble validation RMSE is 3.3 bps of strike against its 20,000-path training labels; re-priced against 400,000-path rough Bergomi references at its hardest smiles (one to five days at 10% vol) the price RMSE is 1.9 to 3.8 bps.

The rough-volatility signature: how fast the skew steepens

The market-facing reason to use rough volatility for short-dated options is the term structure of the at-the-money skew: rough models predict that the skew ψ(T) = dσimp/dk steepens like a power law TH−½ as expiry approaches, which classical stochastic volatility cannot reproduce. Measured from the committed SPY option captures (8 captures across two trading days, 7 expiries each, 56 local-quadratic fits between 2 and 10 trading days), the market exponent is −0.249 ± 0.033, against H − ½ = −0.239 for H = 0.261, the value in the SPY calibration record the experiment reads (the served 0DTE checkpoint's own fit has H = 0.255, whose H − ½ = −0.245 is also within one standard error of the market). The model side is measured the same way, by Monte Carlo with 8 seeds × 400,000 paths per maturity: at that fit's vol-of-vol (η = 3.9) the model's skew is steeper than the asymptotic law (exponent −0.321 ± 0.007) because ηTH is already 0.9 at one day, while at η = 0.5 the same engine returns −0.233 ± 0.011 with an amplitude within 0.4% of the first-order theory. Bitcoin, for contrast, shows no power law at all: its front-month skew is positive.

The term structure also identifies the parameters better than a single day's smile. A smile calibration never sees it explicitly, and buys short-dated curvature most cheaply with more vol-of-vol, which is why that fit's η sits at its bound. Refitting (H, η, ρ, ξ) jointly against the smiles and the per-expiry ATM skew, on a 12 × 12 profile over (H, η) with the remaining parameters optimised at every cell, moves the optimum to H = 0.31, ρ = −0.50 and a forward vol of 12.5 % (bootstrap: H 0.308 ± 0.011) and puts the Monte Carlo skew exponent at −0.264 ± 0.007, within half a market standard error of −0.249, for a cost of 0.45 vol points of smile fit (1.31 to 1.76). A solution with η off its bound reaches the same exponent for 0.15 vol points more. The served short-dated model was trained on an earlier accepted fit (H = 0.255) and is unchanged; the refit is recorded as an analysis artifact.

An arbitrage-free implied-volatility surface

A network that outputs option prices can be smooth and still wrong in a way no market allows: nothing stops it from pricing a butterfly spread below zero or a longer option below a shorter one. Audited by automatic differentiation on 134,100 points across its trained box, the 0DTE pricing ensemble does exactly that at the edges: its implied total variance violates the Durrleman butterfly condition g(k) ≥ 0 on 4.9% of the points where an implied vol exists and the calendar condition on 10.5%, concentrated deep in-the-money at very low volatility and one to two days to expiry; inside the region where its implied vol is resolved by vega, violations are rare (0.06% and 0.18%). The surface served on the dashboard is a second, small network (12,929 parameters) that models total implied variance as a positive output times a Black-Scholes prior and is trained against the ensemble with autograd penalties on both conditions and on the large-moneyness slope, following Ackerer, Tagasovska and Vatter (2020). On the same 134,100 points it has zero violations (minimum g +0.072, minimum ∂w/∂T +6.5×10−5), never prices below intrinsic value, and reproduces the ensemble to 0.22 vol points where the implied vol is resolved. It inherits the ensemble's short-end error against a 400,000-path Monte Carlo of the true dynamics (up to 3 vol points at one day and 10% vol), so it is arbitrage-free, not more accurate. The dashboard evaluates both conditions on the displayed grid for every setting of σ and r.

Heston as the classical reference

Every neural number above is checked against Monte Carlo, whose error is statistical. A semi-analytic reference has none: backend/quant/heston.py prices European options under Heston (1993) by the COS method of Fang and Oosterlee (2008) with the characteristic function in the numerically stable form of Albrecher and coauthors, and reproduces the published reference value 5.785155450 to 8×10−9, satisfies put-call parity to 10−11, recovers Black-Scholes in the zero-vol-of-vol limit to 10−13, and agrees with an independent Euler Monte Carlo within one standard error, pricing a 100-strike smile in 3.6 ms. Calibrated to the same SPY captures as the rough Bergomi map, Heston fits the two-to-ten-day smiles slightly better (0.85 versus 1.10 vol points RMSE over five captures) but only by pushing the mean-reversion speed to its 100-per-year bound with a vol-of-variance near 4.7, and its at-the-money skew then steepens into expiry with exponent −0.66 against the market's −0.25, twice too fast; with mean reversion capped at 10 per year the fit worsens to 1.11 vol points and the exponent is −0.45. Textbook Heston parameters give the classical flat short-dated skew (exponent near 0). A single-day smile does not identify the model; the term structure of the skew does.

Deep hedging and the CVaR objective

Following Buehler and coauthors (2019), a policy network maps the hedging state to a position and is trained to minimize the 95% conditional value at risk of the terminal loss, with transaction costs inside the objective. The Hedging tab simulates 15,000 paths (five seeds of 3,000) of hedging a short 30-day at-the-money call to expiry, and shows the learned policy's profit-and-loss distribution against a vol-matched Black-Scholes delta hedge and a cost-aware Whalley-Wilmott no-trade band on the same paths. On Black-Scholes dynamics the learned policy does not beat either baseline on tail risk; under rough volatility with transaction costs it reaches a lower tail loss than the delta hedge, mostly by trading less, which is the experiment below. Replayed over eight years of real SPY and BTC daily history with only ex-ante information, the default hedger.pt checkpoint (neither of the two the dashboard serves, and byte-identical to the retired legacy-measure checkpoint) keeps its cost efficiency (better mean P&L at 50 bp of cost on 69% of SPY windows, trading about half as much as delta) but not its tail advantage. Neither served policy has been replayed on history; the paired tables and that provenance are in docs/hedging_real_paths.md.

Deep hedging under rough volatility, jumps and costs

On Black-Scholes paths the market is complete and a delta hedge is close to optimal, so a learned policy has little to win there. The dashboard's Deep Hedging tab therefore simulates the SPY-calibrated rough Bergomi model with compensated jumps (H = 0.25, ρ = −0.55, an incomplete market with stochastic rough volatility correlated with spot) and runs three hedgers on identical paths: the learned policy, a vol-matched Black-Scholes delta, and a Whalley-Wilmott no-trade band tuned in the baseline's favour. A fourth, a linear-regression hedge in the style of Ruf and Wang, is reported in docs/deep_hedging_regimes.md. Across 15,000 paths per cost the learned policy's tail loss crosses below the delta hedge at 50 bp of proportional cost (CVaR95 404 ± 7 versus 493 ± 11 bp of strike) and below the Whalley-Wilmott band at 100 bp (452 ± 8 versus 498 ± 10), reaching half the delta hedge's tail loss at 200 bp. It does so by holding about 0.23 less than the Black-Scholes delta, the under-hedge that minimum-variance theory predicts when spot and volatility are negatively correlated, and by trading a third of the delta hedge's turnover. Most of that gap is the smaller commission bill rather than a better-shaped tail: CVaR95 splits exactly into the mean loss and the tail about that mean, and at 50 bp of cost 78.5 of the 88.5 bp advantage over the delta hedge is mean and 10.0 bp is tail shape. On demeaned tail alone the Whalley-Wilmott band is ahead below 200 bp. The same policy evaluated on Black-Scholes paths loses, as it should: the edge is specific to the dynamics it was trained on.

Explainability by integrated gradients

The feature-attribution panel runs Integrated Gradients through the ensemble against an at-the-money baseline, so each input is credited with its share of the price difference from that baseline. The completeness check, that the attributions sum to the price difference, is reported alongside, so a bad attribution shows up as a visible residual rather than disappearing into the chart.

Real market data

Everything above runs on simulated data. The exception is a live Deribit option chain: 836 BTC instruments across 12 expiries, fetched from public endpoints with no API key and committed as a snapshot so the diagnostics run offline. Reading the coin-denominated premium on the per-expiry forward and inverting Black-76 reproduces Deribit's own published mark IV to a median of 0.0028 vol points; reading it as a dollar price instead gives 4.62 vol points where the truth is 32.88, wrong by 7.1x and still a smooth, plausible surface. Of 51 static-arbitrage violations that appear on mid prices, zero are executable once the actual bid-ask has to be crossed.

3. Results

Everything here is measured, not asserted. The evaluation script prices a held-out test set against high-precision Monte Carlo references; the model is never scored against its own noisy training labels, because that overstates the error (by 2.4x on the 0DTE model). The test set is a fresh Latin-hypercube draw from the same parameter box as the training set and is labelled by the same simulator, so these figures are interpolation error inside the trained box, not evidence of generalisation beyond it.

Against Levy the ensemble is 33x more accurate over the full trained box. That 33x is an RMSE ratio over a box where volatility runs to 80%. Levy's error is a bias that grows with maturity (0.15 bps at 0.10 years to 7.89 bps at 2.00 years on the grid below), so the ratio depends on where the box is sampled: at a more typical σ = 0.25 the margin narrows to about 5x, 0.72 against 3.65 bps of mean absolute error. The one regime where Levy still wins is where the true price is essentially zero (0.082 versus 0.310 bps), a consequence of the Softplus output floor at prices near zero.

Levy is not the only closed form for an arithmetic Asian, and against the better one the network loses on price. Curran's (1994) conditioning approximation is a rigorous lower bound on the arithmetic-average price, and on a 36-cell grid at σ = 0.25 it is 6.9x more accurate than the ensemble at about an eighth of the latency.

So the case for the surrogate on a Black-Scholes Asian is not price accuracy. It is the rest of the package: all five Greeks by automatic differentiation in the same call, batch throughput that the dashboard measures live on whichever machine is serving, and a training recipe that needs no geometric-conditioning trick and therefore carries over to dynamics where no such closed form exists, which is exactly how the short-dated rough Bergomi ensemble is built. The advantage above is specific to rough volatility with costs. On Black-Scholes paths the market is complete and the classical baselines hold: across a 12-cell sweep of training and evaluation measures on those paths, the learned policy loses to a vol-matched delta hedge in 7 cells and to the Whalley-Wilmott band in 11. Even under rough volatility the band remains the stronger baseline until costs reach 100 bp. The dashboard runs all three hedgers on the same paths and ranks them by tail loss for the run in front of you.

Because they share paths, the dashboard compares them the way the table above does: paired. One bootstrap resample of the paths is drawn per replicate and every hedger is scored on it, so the common path risk cancels inside each replicate and what is left is the sampling error of the comparison itself. Combining two separate error bars instead, hypot(SEa, SEb), would assume the hedgers were independent, and they are the opposite of independent: a path that is bad for one is usually bad for all three. On the served run at 50 bp the paired errors are 20-58 % smaller than the unpaired formula claims, which changes what the page is entitled to say. The learned policy beats the vol-matched delta hedge by $0.94 ± 0.11 and the Ruf-Wang linear hedge by $0.14 ± 0.06, both intervals clear of zero. Against the Whalley-Wilmott band it is $0.10 ± 0.08 behind, an interval that still contains zero: the band leads on the point estimate and wins about 89 % of resamples, and that is as far as 15,000 paths will carry it. Both the Hedging tab and the risk summary read the same intervals, so they cannot disagree about what this run separates.

4. Scope

  • This is a research and portfolio project, not production trading infrastructure. Nothing here is investment advice.
  • The Asian pricer takes a single flat volatility rather than a full surface and carries no arbitrage constraint of its own, and the short-dated pricing ensemble is not arbitrage-free by construction either. The short-dated implied-volatility surface on the dashboard is the separately trained constrained network that is, and the two are compared above.
  • The rough Bergomi model has constant parameters: η, ρ and H are fitted jointly to one smile and none of them varies with maturity, so one fit covers the short-dated range and longer expiries would need their own. The served short-dated checkpoint was trained on a single calibration to SPY quotes captured on 20 August 2026 and is not refit as the market moves. Fitting H against the skew term structure as well moves it (see the joint refit above), but the served model was not retrained on that result.
  • The deep hedger's advantage is regime-specific. On Black-Scholes paths the classical baselines win; under rough volatility with transaction costs it beats the delta hedge, and the Whalley-Wilmott band from 100 bp; on eight years of real SPY and BTC daily history it keeps its cost efficiency (better mean P&L at 50 bp of cost on 69% of SPY windows) but not its tail advantage. The GAN market simulator used in early training is not a reliable evaluation measure, so no result is reported on it.
  • The served pricer favours price accuracy: its delta and vega are 5.9% and 1.2% less accurate than an alternative head that prices slightly worse. A service hedging off these Greeks should weigh that differently from a service pricing with them.
  • The second-order Greeks are not hedging-grade. An autograd audit of the served Asian ensemble over 120,800 points of its own trained box finds gamma negative on 18.6% of it and vega negative on 28.1%, concentrated where the average is nearly deterministic and the true price is a hinge with no curvature to reproduce; inside the region where a reference vega resolves at all, 0.06% of convexity checks fail and every other condition is clean. Gamma's held-out error against the conditional-density reference is measured above; no hedging experiment in this project runs on the surrogate's own Greeks, since the hedging comparisons use a Black-Scholes delta throughout.
  • The absolute price RMSE moves with the test draw (1.301 to 1.488 bps across four independent draws); only paired comparisons on identical points and references are treated as meaningful.
  • Market data for the ticker feature comes from yfinance, which is retail-grade and has stale or missing quotes. The Deribit chain is a committed snapshot, not a live feed.
  • The live demo runs on a small always-on instance; the Monte Carlo engines simulate in fixed-size blocks of paths so the whole service runs within 512 MB of memory.
